One Anchor and the Shapeless Hour

The Rose Kennedy Greenway at midday smells of grilled onions and river breeze. I picked the food trucks near the Custom House because they serve a purpose that has nothing to do with being picturesque. One turkey-and-cheese, eaten on a bench while the line moved and the pigeons argued over dropped fries. That was the single fixed point. Everything after belonged to the rule.

At 1:17 I stood up, slung the bag higher on my shoulder so the strap wouldn’t tempt my hand, and walked north without deciding which street to take. The tightening arrived almost immediately—the old, efficient reflex that wants to turn an open hour into a private list: the pier, the bookstore two blocks over, the light under the overpass that sometimes works. I felt my breath shorten the way it does when a room suddenly has too many doors. I named it out loud, quietly, the way you speak to a child who is already reaching for the match. Then I kept walking.

The street that claimed me was narrow and half in shadow, the kind that runs behind the old fish exchange buildings. Water still pooled in the brick gutters from an early rain. A woman in a red windbreaker stood at the corner with both hands around a paper cup, not drinking, simply holding the warmth against the wind off the harbor. She didn’t see me. I didn’t lift the camera. The impulse rose and I watched it the same way I had watched the list-making breath—noticed where it wanted to land, then let it pass. The hour wasn’t empty after that; it was simply unsorted. I turned left when a delivery van blocked the crosswalk, followed the sound of a saxophone leaking from an open loading dock, and came out again onto a stretch of the waterfront where the railings were already warm from the sun.

The page I wrote later was small and plain. Greenway anchor noted at the top. 1:17. First turn taken behind the fish exchange on the sound of the saxophone. Camera stayed in the bag because the red windbreaker and the pooled water already felt finished inside the hour and I did not want to finish them twice.

I am testing this rule before Porto because the city will give me more shapeless hours than I can organize, and I would rather meet them as they arrive than spend the week converting them into evidence that I was present. The camera is not the problem. The problem is the quick, anxious translation that turns any stretch of unscheduled time into something that can be shown later, as if the showing were the proof. The rehearsal is small and local and deliberately unphotogenic. That is the entire point. If the rule holds here, it has a chance of holding on the other side of the Atlantic, where the stakes are only that I return with the time itself rather than a collection of images that stand in for having lived it.
