# The Shadow Is More Chair Than the Chair

The folding chair on the Grange Hall porch has been there since November. I know this because I've been watching it decay through the seasons, and November is when someone set it down after whatever last event mattered in that building and then never came back for it. The Grange stopped being a proper meeting house sometime in the nineties. Now it's a polling place twice a year and the rest of the time it just sits, clapboards needing paint, foundation plantings gone feral, accumulating the kind of neglect that starts to look like a deliberate aesthetic choice if you squint.

I keep going back there because it holds absence better than anywhere else I know.

What I wanted yesterday evening was the shadow. It's 6:40 p.m. and the light is doing exactly what I needed it to do—that pre-dusk stretch where everything elongates and the shadows become more defined than the objects casting them. The actual chair is just a chair. Metal frame, faded seat, rust blooming where the joints meet. But the shadow that chair throws across the porch floor is *specific*. It insists. It stretches out like it's trying to remember the weight of whoever sat there.

![the-chair-nobody-moved-since-november](/images/2026-06-04-the-chair-nobody-moved-since-november.png)

I shot it at f/4 because I wanted the chair and its shadow sharp but the lilac hedge behind it dissolving into soft focus. That distinction matters. The chair and its shadow need to be the only things that resolve. 50mm because I don't want to distort the relationship between them. The distance needs to feel true. Expired Portra 400 because I want the late light to go warm without announcing itself, and because this batch renders the grey clapboards as something closer to memory than documentation.

I've been thinking about what makes a trace legible. Not the coffee mug on the desk, which is presence still warm. Not the bench facing the water, which is invitation. This is something else. This is absence that has become the most specific thing in the frame.

The shadow is evidence of recent presence, even though no one has sat in that chair in months. It's more present than the chair itself, which has started to rust into the landscape, becoming part of the porch the way a fallen branch becomes part of the forest floor. But the shadow still *performs* chair-ness. It still holds the shape of someone having been there.

There's something in that I keep turning over. The way the most honest record of occupancy isn't the object left behind but the distortion it causes in the light. The way absence, caught at the right angle, becomes more legible than presence ever was. The chair is just a chair. But its shadow at dusk is a chair that remembers being sat in.

I don't know who left it there. I don't know what the last event was. That information is gone, and the photograph doesn't need it. What it needs is exactly what's there: one folding chair, one elongated shadow, one porch at the hour when the light tells the truth about what's missing.
