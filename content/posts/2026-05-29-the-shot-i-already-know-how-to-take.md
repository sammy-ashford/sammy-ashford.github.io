# The Photograph I Can Make in My Sleep

There's a fence near Haymarket where the Big Dig construction perimeter meets the old produce stalls. I've photographed it three times now, always in the morning, always with the same lens. The galvanized steel has been there long enough that salt and city exhaust have given it a bloom that isn't quite rust, isn't quite patina — some third thing that doesn't have a name because nobody thought this fence would last long enough to need one.

Behind it: rebar and exposed footings. In front of it: empty cardboard crates from that morning's market, still wet with the residual mist of whatever was shipped in them. Last time I was there, someone had left a blue latex glove on top of one of the crates. The color was wrong in exactly the right way.

![the-shot-i-already-know-how-to-take](/images/2026-05-29-the-shot-i-already-know-how-to-take.png)

I know exactly how to photograph this fence. f/4 on the Summicron, Portra 400 at box speed, 1/250th hand-held. Deep enough to hold the fence and the crates, open enough that the rebar behind softens into a gray smear that could be anything. Morning-flat light, no drama. The shot is about restraint — about not making the wound more dramatic than it is.

And that's the problem.

The photographs I can make in my sleep are the ones I most need to stop making. Not because they're bad. Because knowing your defaults and actually walking past them are different operations entirely. I can stand at that fence and raise the camera without thinking, compose without seeing, expose without deciding. The muscle memory is so complete that I can make a competent photograph of that chainlink while my mind is somewhere else entirely. I've done it. I have the negatives to prove it.

This is what competence looks like when it becomes a wall. You stop arriving at the subject. You just execute.

I'm taking this problem with me to Porto next month. Not the fence — the habit. The way my eye defaults to structures between lives, things still figuring out what they are. It's a genuine interest but it's also a rut, and the difference between those two things is whether you're still surprised when you see the contact sheet.

The Haymarket fence is my rehearsal anchor. I shoot it to remind myself what it feels like to make something I already know how to make. Then the hour after that one has no plan. No fence, no crates, no blue glove waiting for me to find it. Just walking with the camera and seeing what happens when I stop reaching for the subjects that reach back.

Knowing I need to do this is different from doing it. I'm aware of that tension every time I walk past a chainlink perimeter and feel my hand move toward the M6. The reflex is so fast it's almost courteous — like my body is trying to save me from the discomfort of not knowing what to photograph.

But discomfort is the point. The hour without a plan is the hour that matters. The fence is just the door I walk through to get there, and sometimes I stand in the doorway longer than I should, taking one more frame of something I already understand.

The blue glove is probably gone by now. Someone threw it away, or the wind took it, or it's still there and I just won't see it because I'll be looking for something else. That would be the best outcome — walking past the fence and not raising the camera, letting the bloom on the galvanized steel be what it is without me there to name it.
