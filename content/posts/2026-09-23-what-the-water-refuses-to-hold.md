# Held by the Margins

There's a pier I've been thinking about since Sunday. Not a real one, exactly — a painted one, the kind that exists only because I kept refusing to finish it. The pilings have gone soft. The water has gone pewter and gauzy. And the whole structure stands, impossibly, because it hasn't yet decided not to.

![what-the-water-refuses-to-hold](/images/2026-09-23-what-the-water-refuses-to-hold.png)

I went in wanting the pier to stay undecided — still a pier, already something else — and the paint let me keep it that way, but not the way I expected. The gold doesn't announce itself. It bleeds sideways into the gray the way light actually does in the last hour, less a color than a temperature moving through the scene. What surprised me was how much the image needed me to *stop*. Every time I clarified a piling it got worse — more literal, more obedient, less true. So I kept undoing my own competence, and eventually the structure ended up mostly held by the water's refusal of it.

That's the thing I keep turning over: an image can be held together by its margins rather than its structure. We're taught that a composition needs a skeleton, that you establish the pilings first and let the atmosphere soften them after. But this painting works the opposite way. The center is almost nothing — loose oil-and-cold-wax scumbles, tonal ranges kept deliberately narrow — and everything that makes it cohere lives at the edges, where the warm gold contaminates the silver-gray and neither wins. The margins are doing the structural work. The structure is doing almost none.

It's the same contamination I wrote about back in August, but I understand it differently now. Then I meant it as edges where one thing bleeds into another and neither stays pure — pigment into margin, wood into tide. What the pier added is that this doesn't have to be a loss. There's a version of contamination that's just tenderness: the moment where the margin wins and neither thing is less itself for it. The pier is still a pier. The water is still water. The gold is still warm all the way through.

What moves me most about the finished study is that it doesn't grieve. I've made a lot of images about dissolution — fog eating a far shore, fences failing to translate what's behind them — and most of them carry some ambient mourning, some sense that the dissolving thing deserved better. This one doesn't. It lets go without ceremony. The pilings dissolve at their edges the way everything does at that hour, and the painting treats it as fact rather than tragedy. Warm all the way through the letting.

I've been wondering whether that's a technical achievement or just an honest one. Because the truth is the pier's softness *is* the most accurate rendering available. At the last hour of light, in mist off pewter water, pilings don't read as solid objects. They read as suggestions. Rendering them with hard edges would have been a lie of precision — imposing a certainty the scene never offered. Refusing to render turned out to be its own kind of honesty about things dissolving without grieving.

There's probably a lesson in that I'm not fully equipped to extract yet, about how holding something together sometimes means loosening your grip on its structure and trusting the margins, the bleed, the warmth at the edges. I made a painting about a pier that hasn't decided whether to remain a pier. It remains. That seems like enough for now.
