# The Third Thing the Grass Makes

There's a jetty off a beach access road in Old Lyme that I've been thinking about since last month. Granite riprap, eighty-some years of tide, edges gone soft the way only stone that's been told "no" ten thousand times a day can go soft. In September the marsh grass grows up through the cracks until you genuinely cannot say where the structure ends and the field begins. I went out around 6:40 to catch the last light — that brief window when the Sound goes pewter and the Spartina catches a color that isn't quite gold and isn't quite green, which is a way of saying the grass was already contaminating the light before I ever pointed a camera at it.

![where-the-jetty-forgets-it-is-stone](/images/2026-09-15-where-the-jetty-forgets-it-is-stone.png)

The frame I wanted: the jetty receding diagonally into flat water, grass colonizing the stone, no horizon drama. The water dull on purpose — a ground for the contamination to happen against. What I keep circling back to is the idea that when two things bleed into each other, neither is ruined. They make a third thing. The jetty doesn't stop being a jetty; the marsh doesn't stop being marsh. But at the seam, where the roots have cracked the granite and the granite has constrained the roots, there's something that is only itself — interleaved, neither-nor, and better for it.

I shot at 1/60, braced on the riprap, specifically because the wind was moving the grass tips. F/11 would have been the responsible choice. But I wanted those tips just barely smearing. The blur is the contamination made visible. A crisp grass blade is a grass blade; a smeared one is a grass blade *becoming* something, and the camera — which is supposed to arrest time — is caught in the act of failing to do its one job, and the failure is the whole picture.

And I shot Portra 400, which is a small hypocrisy I've made peace with. I spent years wanting faithful color, wanting the film to be a window. Now I want the film to be a participant. Portra's greens drift. Its shadows go muddy-brown. It does its own quiet contaminating of every scene it touches, and I chose it for exactly that reason. The medium format frame holds the near grass and the mid-jetty crisp while the far water softens, echoing the way the light itself was degrading minute by minute. Depth of field as metaphor is a cheap move, I know. The 75mm stacking the grass against the stone so the interleaving reads dense — that's not a metaphor, that's just compression doing honest work. But the two together, sharp-to-soft and interleaved-to-blurred, say the thing I couldn't say any other way: the boundary was failing, and the failure was the beauty.

I've written before about the scumble — the painter's move where you drag opaque paint over wet paint and neither layer submits. I keep returning to it because I keep finding it. The jetty is just the latest instance. The structure outlasting its function, the marsh quietly taking it over, neither one winning — that's not decay. Decay implies something is being lost. Here nothing is lost. Something is being *added*: the third thing, the interleaved thing, the thing that only exists because the boundary gave out.

One frame that mattered. Then I walked the shoreline until the light was gone and nothing else insisted, which is its own kind of honesty. You don't take the third thing home in multiples. You get one.
