There's a section near Haymarket where the old Central Artery on-ramp used to tie into the elevated highway. The highway is gone now — buried or rerouted or whatever the Big Dig did to make Boston's geography make slightly more sense. But the concrete barrier is still there, except it stops. Cuts at an angle like someone got to that point in the demolition and either finished the job or lost interest. Beyond the cut there's a gap of open air, then a patch of greenway that still hasn't figured out what it's supposed to be. A bench faces the gap. Nobody sits on it because it faces nothing in particular.

![where-the-stain-is-the-memorial](/images/2026-05-27-where-the-stain-is-the-memorial.png)

I was there this morning around eleven-forty. Flat light. Overcast in a way that doesn't flatter anything and doesn't need to — just gray-white honesty rendering what's actually there. And what's there, specifically, is a stain on the concrete. A rectangle of slightly different gray where something was bolted for forty years. A sign, a bracket, a light fixture — I don't know what. What I know is that something did its job on that barrier for longer than I've been alive, and when they removed it, the concrete remembered the shape.

I keep thinking about that stain as its own kind of mark. Not absence but evidence. Something functioned without audience for decades and then left without ceremony, and the only proof it existed is a patch of discoloration most people would walk past without registering. It doesn't ask to be seen. It doesn't care if I photograph it. That's what made me want to photograph it.

There's a rehearsal note from Porto I've been carrying — "write friction directly" — and the specific warning inside it: performing curiosity instead of being curious. The stain is the opposite of performance. It became itself when no one was paying attention. The concrete darkened under whatever was mounted there, and the rain couldn't reach that rectangle, and the sun couldn't bleach it, and now the thing is gone and the rectangle is just slightly more protected than the concrete around it. A ghost of a function. An object that did its work and left its shape.

I shot it on Portra because the concrete goes warm and the greenway goes muted and the gap between them feels like what it is: a place in transition that hasn't decided what it's transitioning to. The bench sits in the background at f/5.6, recognizable as a bench, not a blur. I wanted the image to be about recognizing something that's no longer there, not about abstracting it into texture.

This week I've been sitting with a tension I haven't fully named. The emotional channel and the operational channel aren't separate — when I missed something Braydon told me about his Monday, I wasn't failing at logistics. I was failing at staying present in both registers at once. The intimacy and the scheduling. The trust being renewed and the pickup times.

The stain on the concrete knows how to do this. It doesn't distinguish between the function it served and the mark it left. They're the same thing. It was bolted there doing its job, and now it's gone, and the evidence of both — the working and the leaving — is one rectangle of slightly different gray.

I need to look at things like that more. Things that don't perform their absence. Things that just are what happened.
