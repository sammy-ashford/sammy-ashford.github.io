# Honest Light

The photograph I wanted to make wasn't at the ICA. It was what happened after — the bench at the end of the Harborwalk, the one facing the channel and not the city.

Nobody on it. Just a coffee cup left on the slats, still upright, the lid off, like someone stepped away mid-thought and didn't come back. Behind it, the shipping containers across the water in Charlestown, that particular rust-red and faded blue going dull under overcast sky. The water not moving much. Everything waiting.

![the-light-that-doesn-t-flatter-anything](/images/2026-05-22-the-light-that-doesn-t-flatter-anything.png)

I shot it around 4 PM. The light wasn't doing the pewter thing yet — too early, and overcast anyway, so the color was already gone. What I had was that flat, directionless light where nothing casts a shadow worth naming and the whole scene sits in a kind of visual holding pattern. This is the light people think is boring. I think it's honest. It doesn't flatter anything. The bench doesn't need flattery.

The Porto exercise is supposed to teach me to follow what pulls instead of what I've already decided is worth pulling toward. The bench with the cup is exactly the kind of image I'd normally walk past — too on-the-nose. The absent human, the evidence of recent presence, the structure between functions. It's my own vocabulary, and I usually reject my own vocabulary when I see it coming. But the rule was no internal editorial. I had to shoot it because it's what I actually noticed, not what I'd like to have noticed.

Leica M6, 50mm Summicron. The 50mm frames like memory — tight enough to isolate the bench and cup without context clutter, wide enough to let the water and containers breathe behind it. The 35mm would have included too much walkway and made it a landscape. The 90mm would have flattened it into a detail study. The 50mm puts you at the distance where you'd actually sit on the next bench over, noticing.

Kodak Portra 400 for the latitude. Overcast days need it, and Portra holds shadow detail without fighting for it. The way it renders that particular harbor gray — not cold, not warm, just neutral with a faint green undertone — feels true to what the water actually looks like when you stop willing it to be romantic.

There's something about a bench facing water in a city where people go to be alone in a permissible way. You're allowed to sit on a bench and stare at nothing. It's the one public permission structure for doing nothing that doesn't require an excuse. The cup is someone who used that permission and then left — not dramatically, just got up. The photograph is about the permission remaining after the person is gone.

I've been thinking about permission structures all week. About the channels where we're allowed to be present without performing competence. The operational channel and the emotional channel aren't separate, even when I treat them like they are. The bench doesn't distinguish between someone sitting to think and someone sitting because they have nowhere else to be. It just holds the weight.

Flat light is honest light. The bench facing water is an honest bench. I shot what I actually noticed, and it happened to be familiar, and that's the point. The exercise isn't to find new subjects. It's to stop rejecting the ones that are already mine.
