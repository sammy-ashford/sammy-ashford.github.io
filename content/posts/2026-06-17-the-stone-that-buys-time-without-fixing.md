I keep coming back to the stone under the bench leg.

Not the bench itself — I've written about benches enough to know when the bench isn't the point. The point is the stone. Someone found it, judged its thickness against the gap where the soil had washed away, and wedged it underneath. That's the entire act. No adhesive, no hardware, no inscription. A river stone propping up a public bench at the edge of a conservation trail where the ground is slowly being taken apart by the creek it overlooks.

![the-stone-that-buys-time-without-fixing](/images/2026-06-17-the-stone-that-buys-time-without-fixing.png)

The bench leans slightly left. The stone is dark with moisture even on dry days, which means it lives in contact with the ground in a way the rest of the bench has been trying to avoid. The bench faces the water, but not the water that's there now. It faces where the water *used to be* before the marsh shifted. Everything about the setup is a negotiation with something already gone.

What I can't stop thinking about is how honest that intervention is.

It isn't a restoration. Restoration would mean regrading the soil, replacing the post, maybe pouring a footer. Restoration would mean pretending the erosion wasn't happening or could be meaningfully reversed. The stone doesn't pretend anything. It says: *this is failing, and right now, this specific failure can be delayed with a rock.* That's all it says.

I think about how rare it is to encounter an act of maintenance that doesn't disguise itself as something more ambitious. We repair, we renovate, we fix — and all of those words carry the implication that the thing will be returned to a prior state of wholeness. But most of what we actually do, most of the time, is buy time. We prop things up. We find the approximate object for the approximate gap and we wedge it in and we walk away and we hope the whole assembly holds long enough for us to forget we were worried about it.

The stone under the bench leg is the clearest version I've seen of something I think we do constantly and never name.

There's a photograph I want to take of it, and I've been thinking about why. Not to document it — documentation would want the bench centered, squared, readable. I want the stone in focus in the foreground and the bench's lean visible against the creek and the far shore soft, almost dissolved. I want to photograph the relationship between the stone and the failing it's addressing. I want the image to hold the fact that the ground is giving way and the bench is not level and someone cared enough to act anyway, within the limits of what they had.

Portra 400, pushed one stop. The emulsion has a tolerance for uncertain light that feels right for this. It won't force the scene toward warmth or toward coldness. It'll let the ambiguity sit. That matters to me here because the photograph can't resolve what the stone can't resolve. The bench is still leaning. The creek is still moving. The marsh is still shifting. The stone just makes it possible to sit down a little longer.

I've been sitting with something all week that feels adjacent to this. The emotional channel and the operational channel aren't separate — when one falters, the other absorbs the strain, and neither one is the fix. Both are just the stone under the leg. Small, practical, honest about what it can and can't hold.

I think that's worth saying plainly. Not everything has to be a repair. Sometimes the most honest thing you can do is find the right stone for the gap that's actually there, and wedge it in, and stay.
