# The Pier That Ends Partway Out

I went to the old ferry landing on the Old Saybrook side tonight at 7:40, which is a very specific time to arrive anywhere, but I'd been watching the light all week and I knew the hour I wanted. The hour before dusk, when the Sound goes pewter and the pilings lose their edges. If you show up too early the water is still performing blueness. Too late and everything goes purple and sentimental. There's a window, and it's narrow, and I have learned to respect it.

The ferry stopped running before I was born. The pilings have been rotting in an order that isn't alphabetical — some still wear faded barn-red, some have gone gray to the waterline, some are just suggestions of themselves — and the decking ends mid-span where somebody, decades ago, finally gave up maintaining it. It doesn't reach the other shore. It doesn't reach anything. It goes out over the water and then it stops.

![the-pier-that-stopped-short-of-far-shore](/images/2026-08-22-the-pier-that-stopped-short-of-far-shore.png)

Here is what I keep coming back to: it's not a ruin. A ruin has surrendered. This pier hasn't. It's been standing there long enough that nobody alive remembers what it was for, which means it's become whatever it is now. The pilings still walk out toward the horizon in diminishing perspective, still holding formation, still holding posture — and posture, it turns out, doesn't require a destination. It only requires that you keep standing the way you learned to stand.

I've been sitting with translation all week, professionally and otherwise. What happens to a thing when it has to cross into a grammar that has no tense for it. A pier that used to mean *crossing* and now means *ending partway out over the water* is the same story in wood. The structure survives the loss of its purpose. The structure might even be the thing that outlasts the purpose, and keeps something of it — not the function but the form the function made. The reaching is still visible in the geometry even after the arriving is gone.

I shot low, from the beach, so the deck line cuts the frame about a third up and the pilings recede in order. f/11, because I wanted the nearest piling and the farthest to carry equal weight — the whole system deserves to be in focus, not just its front edge. 80mm, no wide-angle drama. The subject doesn't need editorializing. I metered for the water and let the pilings go slightly dark, read as masses rather than details, because that's how they actually read at that hour and I wasn't interested in rescuing them into clarity they don't have.

One compositional decision mattered more than the rest: I let the end of the pier fall just short of the frame's edge. You can't see where it stops. The incompleteness is felt rather than shown. I think about the difference a lot. Shown incompleteness asks you to pity the thing. Felt incompleteness asks you to stand at the railing with it.

One frame, maybe two, and then I put the camera away before the light turned pretty. There's a moment after that window closes when everything goes gorgeous in a way that flatters the photographer and lies about the subject. The pier deserved better than that. It's spent eighty years not being pretty, and it's become something better: certain of itself, unfinished, and completely uninterested in your sympathy.

I walked back along the beach in the last of it. The water was calm enough that the pilings' reflections held as definite forms, each one doubled patiently in the pewter. Everything incomplete, everything still standing. I think that's the whole lesson, if it is one, and I'm content to let it sit in the car on the way home.
