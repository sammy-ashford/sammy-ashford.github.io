# The Back of the Building Keeps the Records

There's a loading dock behind the dry cleaner on Fifth that I've probably walked past two hundred times without ever once looking at it. That's the thing about the backs of buildings — they exist in our peripheral vision as connective tissue, the gaps between destinations. You pass through the alley the way you pass through a doorway, registering nothing. The storefront faces the street and gets all the attention: the sign, the window display, the bell over the door. The back is where the work happens, where the steam vents out at ankle height and the asphalt stays dark even in dry weather, and nobody looks at it because nobody is supposed to.

I've started to think that's backwards. The front of a building is what it wants to be. The back is what it is.

![where-the-steam-catches-the-low-light](/images/2026-10-25-where-the-steam-catches-the-low-light.png)

I went back there yesterday at 4:40 specifically for the light. It comes in low over the roofline of the building across the alley, raking across the dock at maybe twenty-five degrees, and when the steam catches it, it reads as a soft diagonal sheet while everything else falls into flat shadow. That contrast is the entire picture. On an overcast day this would be nothing — the steam is only the subject because the light makes it visible. Which is its own small lesson: most of what's worth photographing is worth photographing for about forty minutes a day, and the rest of the time it's just a loading dock.

What I actually wanted in the frame wasn't the steam at all. It was the folding chair one of the workers keeps by the door, and the coffee can ashtray on the ledge. Evidence of a person without a person. I didn't want to photograph the workers — that would have felt like taking something. I wanted to photograph the pause they'd built for themselves, the small domestic architecture of a break. A chair positioned just so, an ashtray at arm's reach: those are decisions, made repeatedly, by someone whose day includes this exact pause. The photograph is a record of the record they're already keeping.

Technically it was a 50mm situation. The alley is narrow enough that anything wider would distort the dock edges, and the compression at 50 keeps the steam column reading as a plane rather than a smudge. f/8 so the chair, the vent, and the brick texture all stay legible. 1/250 — the steam moves fast, and slower speeds blur it into fog, losing the striations that make it interesting. I wanted it stopped but not frozen stiff: the outer wisps soft, the core defined. Portra 400 for the warm brick and whatever color the steam picks up off the late sun.

I metered for the lit steam and let everything else fall two stops down. Took maybe eight frames as the sun dropped and the angle changed. The right one was not the first one — it never is. It was the frame where the chair's shadow just touched the vent. I stood there waiting for that geometry for the better part of ten minutes, which is a long time to stand in an alley behind a dry cleaner, and a worker came out at one point and looked at me with an expression I'd describe as tolerant. He didn't ask. I think he understood that I was waiting for something, even if he didn't know what, and that waiting for something in an alley is a thing a person might reasonably do.

The argument I'm trying to make in one frame is this: the back of things — the work side, the venting-out side — is where the day actually accumulates. The steam is the day's residue. The chair is where someone sat inside that residue. Every building keeps two sets of books, and only one of them faces the street.
