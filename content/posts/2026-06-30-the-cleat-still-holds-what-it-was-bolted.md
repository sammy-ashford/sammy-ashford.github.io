There's a concrete seawall along the Sound where someone bolted an iron cleat — probably sixty years ago, maybe longer. The cleat is still solid. You could tie a line to it today and it would hold. The concrete around it has been spalling for decades, and now there's a fissure running from the base of the cleat out toward the waterline. Salt water gets in, freezes, opens it a little more each year. The cleat holds. The thing it was bolted *to* is what's giving way.

I keep returning to that inversion. It's the whole story, isn't it? The component designed to bear strain is fine. The structure designed to support the component is the thing failing. And the failure isn't dramatic — no collapse, no sudden event. Just a crack widening one freeze cycle at a time, granular and patient, while the cleat sits there doing exactly what it was installed to do.

I got there around 6:40 last Saturday. The sun had already moved behind the Connecticut shore, and the light does this thing on the Sound that shouldn't work but does — it goes flat and warm at the same time. The water was pewter, trending darker. The cleat and the cracked concrete held the last warm light for about twelve minutes. Then everything went grayscale.

I brought the Hasselblad. The 80mm Planar. There's a reason I keep reaching for that lens when the subject is this direct. It records what's there at roughly the scale the eye accepts as honest. No wide-angle drama, no telephoto compression. Just the thing, at its actual size, in its actual place. f/8, because I needed the cleat sharp and I needed the crack sharp — that relationship *is* the photograph — but I wanted the water behind it to go soft. At roughly six feet, f/8 gives me a sliver of focus that holds both the foreground concrete texture and the cleat's curve while letting the Sound dissolve into tone.

1/60. The water registers as a slow blur. Not silk, not frozen. Movement acknowledging its own pace. The cleat and concrete are perfectly still. That contrast matters more than I can explain. What holds is still. What moves is what's been moving since before the cleat was placed.

Tri-X rated at 250. The half-stop of latitude in the shadows let me read the texture inside that fissure — where the concrete has gone granular and weak, where you can see the aggregate showing through like bone through skin — without blowing the highlights on the iron. The grain gives the surface a physicality that a finer film would flatten. I wanted grit to read as grit.

The composition is simple. Cleat slightly left of center, low. The crack running diagonally from lower right up toward it. The horizon — water, distant shore haze — sitting in the upper third, slightly tilted, because the seawall isn't level and I wasn't going to pretend it was.

What I was after, if I'm being precise: the specific dignity of a thing still doing its job within a system that has stopped being the system it was built for. Nobody ties up boats here anymore. The cleat doesn't know that. It's still bolted to something. The something is failing. And when the concrete finally gives, the cleat will outlast it — and then it will mean something else entirely. Just iron. Just shape. Just a parable about what remains after the context that gave you purpose has eroded out from under you.

I've been sitting with that longer than I expected to.
