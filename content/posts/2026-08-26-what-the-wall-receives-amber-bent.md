# The Wall Has No Tense

The window at the top of the stairs faces west, and the glass in it has a wave in it that nobody has ever replaced. I don't know why nobody replaced it. Probably the usual reason: it stopped being a defect and became the house. At around 6:40 in the evening, in late August, the sun drops low enough to come through that glass at an angle, and the neighbor's maple gets cast onto the plaster wall of the landing — bent, warped, swimming. Amber where the light lands. Blue-gray in the corners where the room is just being a room.

I've been writing all week about translation between grammars that don't share a tense — about what happens when something crosses from one register into another and arrives changed. And then I stood on the landing Tuesday evening and realized the window has been making this argument the whole time. Whatever's outside — the tree, the power line, the light itself — gets refracted on its way through the old glass, and what the wall receives is not the tree. It's a rendering of the tree. The wall has no access to the original. It only gets the translation.

![what-the-wall-receives-amber-bent](/images/2026-08-26-what-the-wall-receives-amber-bent.png)

That's what I wanted to photograph. Not the maple. The maple is doing fine and needs nothing from me. What I wanted was the cast — the warped amber version of a tree on a plaster wall in a house that used to be formal, the room's decline visible in the cool gray around it, holding both registers at once: the warm translated image and the plain faded room it arrives into.

I shot it on the Rolleiflex, square format, because the shot is architectural and still and I didn't want the frame to favor either party — the window's edge or the wall's distortion. f/8, so the window frame and the cast sit in the same focus plane. That mattered to me more than it probably should have. The point is that glass and plaster are one continuous act: source to rendering, no gap where you could insert an original.

The exposure was a quarter of a second, and there was wind, and the maple moved. So the cast shivered. At 1/15 that means smear — the leaves ghosted rather than resolved. I bracketed. One crisp, one blurred. And I keep coming back to the blurred one, because I think it's the truer version. A feeling crossing into a grammar with no tense for it doesn't arrive sharp. It arrives smeared, approximate, warm where the light caught it and vague at the edges. The crisp frame is more impressive. The smeared frame is more honest.

I had maybe fifteen minutes of that light. Then it went pewter and the argument was over for the day.

What I keep turning over is that the wall isn't diminished by receiving only the translation. The plaster doesn't sit there wishing it could know the actual tree. It holds what came through — warped, amber, wrong in every measurable particular — and that wrongness is the whole relationship. The wall and the tree know each other only through the glass, and the glass is old and wavy and slightly dishonest, and somehow that's the condition for their knowing each other at all.

I've been sitting with that, this week, in other contexts. The channels I have for reaching people are all refracting media. Everything I send arrives bent. Everything sent to me arrives bent. And the alternative isn't a crisp version — there is no windowless wall, no unmediated tree. The alternative is just darkness on plaster. I'd rather have the warped amber smear. I'd rather be translated than unreceived.

The light came through for about fifteen minutes and then the street's roofs took it. The cast faded, and the wall went back to being a wall, and the tree went back to being outside, and the only evidence was a negative I haven't developed yet and this. Which is also a translation. Warm where the light caught it. Vague at the edges.
