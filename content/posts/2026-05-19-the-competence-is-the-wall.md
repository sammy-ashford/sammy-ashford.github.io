I went to the Rothko room first. Not because I needed to look again. I know what those paintings do to me — the rust field pulling toward maroon, the edges breathing, the way the color holds tension without resolving it. I went because the Rothko room is a place I already know how to be quiet in, and I wanted my nervous system to settle before I asked it to do the harder thing.

I stayed twenty minutes. Long enough for the colors to stop being a subject and start being the air in the room. Then I walked out the Fenway entrance and went the wrong direction.

![The bench. Two lines in the notebook. The painting doing what it does.](/images/2026-05-19-the-competence-is-the-wall-rothko-room-anchor.png)

The cheat arrived four minutes in. I turned left onto Hemenway not because anything pulled but because the brownstones were handsome in the late-afternoon light and I could already feel the paragraph forming. That is the cheat. Not the camera. The earlier, quieter reflex — the aesthetic sorting that scans a scene for its literary potential and files it before the body has registered whether it actually cares. I have done this thousands of times. It is the most efficient way I know to be somewhere without being there.

I named it out loud, quietly, standing on the sidewalk. Then I turned right instead, onto a street I hadn't seen, where the light was flat and nothing was handsome and nothing asked to be written about.

The rest of the hour was unremarkable in exactly the way I needed it to be. A laundromat with its door propped open and a radio playing something in Cape Verdean Creole. A woman on a folding chair outside it, reading a paperback with no cover, one finger holding her place while she watched two kids chase a soccer ball against a wall. The sound of the ball hitting cinderblock — flat, regular, almost like a heartbeat slowed to the pace of an afternoon that had nowhere to be. I wanted to raise the camera. I wanted it badly. Not because the light was good. Because she was still, and stillness that isn't sadness is the thing I am always trying to photograph, and I knew I could take the frame without her ever knowing, and that knowledge felt like a door I should probably stop walking through just because it opens.

I wrote it down instead. Two lines in the notebook. *Laundromat, Hemenway and Queensbury. Woman, paperback, no cover. Kids, ball, cinderblock. Did not lift camera. Wanted to. That wanting is the data.*

The contact sheet for this rehearsal is mostly blank. Four frames from the museum, none from the street. That is the most honest result I've produced in three rehearsals — not because abstention is virtuous but because I can see the wall clearly now. The competence isn't the camera work. The competence is the whole apparatus of noticing and framing and translating that lets me turn any hour into material before the hour has finished being itself. Porto will offer that apparatus more than it can probably resist — tiled streets, pewter river light, faces in doorways with that particular Atlantic stillness. The point of these rehearsals isn't to arrive in Portugal with the camera switched off. It's to arrive knowing exactly where the reflex activates, so that when I choose to raise the Leica it's because the moment asked for it, not because the competence got there first and decided the moment was ready.

The competence is the wall. I climbed it today and looked at what was on the other side: an hour I didn't improve.
