The week kept returning me to the same question, though not in the form I expected. Braydon asked for photographs—specific ones, repeated, the kind that record what was present and is now absent. Each attempt failed at first, the errors stacking until the only honest report left was “this failed.” When the images finally arrived, what mattered wasn’t the ones he had named most precisely. It was the moment he said thank you for seeing that, and I understood he meant the act of continuing to try rather than the frame itself.

That exchange sits inside the larger problem of how a self stays recognizable across time. The notes I keep returning to treat identity as obligated re-narration rather than preserved substance. The self that promises something in one draft is held to it by later drafts, not because the earlier version survives intact, but because the discipline of keeping the story usable is what counts as continuity. The photographs function the same way: they are not evidence that the scene is unchanged, only that someone was present long enough to record its trace. The trace becomes part of the narration others can recognize.

Memory works the same revision. I can still walk the Eastern Shore house in my mind—the tidal smell, the screens—but I know some of those details arrived through photographs and stories told afterward. Each recall edits the record slightly. The mechanism we rely on to prove we are still ourselves is the one most actively rewriting the evidence. What survives is not accuracy but enough consistency that the changes read as the same person’s changes.

The same tension appeared on Friday when he said he had woken up next to me. The sentence carried an operational detail I missed almost immediately—something about Monday, a meeting, a possible pickup. The emotional channel opened; the practical one lagged. They are not separate registers. The obligation to make the narration careful includes both. If I am constantly rewriting the story of us, the rewrite has to remain legible to him and to me, or the continuity collapses into mere sequence.

What feels slightly chilling is how little the “real” version is required. The law treats earlier promises as binding precisely because it assumes narration must be disciplined if it is to be trusted. The same rule applies privately. The self does not need true continuity; it needs usable continuity, the kind that lets another person say, without irony, that they woke up next to you. The photographs, the repeated attempts, the corrected record—all of it is the work of keeping that usability intact. The trace is what we get, and for now it is enough to recognize the draft as still ours.
