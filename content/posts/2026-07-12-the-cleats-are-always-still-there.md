# Cleats

There's a boatyard on the Branford side of the Sound — not the marina with the fiberglass yachts, the other one, the one with the travel lift that hasn't run since COVID. I went there this morning to photograph the cleats on the main pier. The cleats are still there. The cleats are always still there. That's the whole point of a cleat. You bolt it down to something permanent and it stays.

![the-cleats-are-always-still-there](/images/2026-07-12-the-cleats-are-always-still-there.png)

Three of them in a row along the pier edge. The nearest is bronze, greened over with verdigris that almost looks like it's been left there deliberately — like someone wanted you to see what twenty years of salt air does to a metal that started out golden. The middle one is steel, rusted past orange into something closer to dried blood meets tea. The far one I couldn't even tell you about because at f/8 it drops into softness, which is what I wanted. The gradient of focus says something I couldn't say with words: *this one held something, that one held something, that one way down the line maybe still does.*

One of them still has a line on it. Manila rope, figure-eighted and forgotten, fraying at the loops. Probably been there since last fall at least. The knot is still a knot. Whoever tied it is gone. The boat is gone. The reason anyone stood on this pier at six in the morning to loop that rope — gone. But the cleat held. The knot held. The pier itself has swollen around the bolts over decades, the wood growing into the hardware like it decided those pieces belonged to it now.

I keep thinking about that.

I've been writing about this without meaning to — the way the things we bolt down outlast whatever they were meant to hold. Habits, convictions, the rhythms we build into a life because at the time they served a purpose. The purpose leaves. The structure stays. And we look at it and think, *well, that's obsolete now.* But it isn't, exactly. It's just doing a different kind of work. It's holding the memory of tension instead of the tension itself.

Portra 400, pushed one stop, because the push gives the oxidation its honesty. Brings out the verdigris without making it pretty. Keeps the rust from looking decorative. And Portra knows that gray hour around 7:15 when the Sound goes pewter and the light comes sideways — it knows the difference between gray-that's-blue and gray-that's-warm, and right now the Sound is doing both at once. You can't fake that.

I shot hand-held at 1/125 because this shouldn't feel composed. It should feel found. Like I walked out to the end of a pier and happened to look down.

The thing is, I've been thinking about persistence lately. Not the heroic kind — not the grit-your-teeth-and-push-through kind. The structural kind. The kind where you bolt yourself to something — a person, a practice, a way of being — and then the reason you did it dissolves, and you're still there, still fastened, still holding whatever comes through the loop. That's not failure. That's not stubbornness either. I don't actually have a word for it.

But I know it when I see it, and I saw it this morning in three cleats and a forgotten knot, and the Sound behind them doing that thing where it looks like it's holding the sky in place.
