There's a bench at the edge of the park where the lawn gives up and the Sound begins. Wood slats, no back, the kind the city installed twenty years ago and has since forgotten about. The paint is almost entirely gone. What remains has gone the color of bone — not white but whatever color wood arrives at after enough seasons of rain and neglect and accidental persistence.

People sit there anyway.

You can tell because the grass in front of it is worn down to bare dirt. Not a path. A semicircle — the exact shape feet make when they rest, shift, scuff against the ground while someone watches the water. The bench doesn't know it's decommissioned. Nobody told it to stop being a bench, and even if they had, I'm not sure it would matter. It keeps receiving people. That's the whole arrangement.

![the-bench-doesn-t-know-it-s-decommission](/images/2026-06-19-the-bench-doesn-t-know-it-s-decommission.png)

I went to photograph it tonight. Six-forty, right at the edge of when the light gives out. I wanted the worn ground visible, the bench empty, the whole frame to feel like a room someone had just stepped out of. Recent absence. The kind of emptiness that implies presence rather than lacking it.

I shot from behind and slightly left. The semicircle of dirt leads the eye toward the water. The bench occupies the lower third. The Sound fills the rest — pewter and soft, the water already losing its color to the sky. 50mm, f/4, 1/60 on a tripod. Portra 400 pushed one stop because Portra handles transitional light without forcing contrast where none exists. The bench goes slightly soft at the edges. The worn ground stays sharp. The water dissolves. Three layers of clarity fading into each other, which is what the light was already doing on its own.

One frame. Maybe two. The bench was already doing the work. I just needed to not get in its way.

What I keep thinking about is the gap between official abandonment and actual use. The city stopped maintaining this bench. At some point a crew drove past and made a quiet decision — not worth replacing, not worth repainting, not worth the administrative effort of removal. It entered a kind of bureaucratic afterlife. Decommissioned in spirit, if not in any formal paperwork anyone could locate.

But none of that reaches the bench itself. And more importantly, none of it reaches the people who sit there. They don't need the city's permission. They don't need a plaque or an inspection sticker. They just need a place at the edge of the water, and this bench provides one, the way it has for two decades, the way objects do when they're allowed to keep being what they are.

Use outlasts intention. That's the thing. Someone designed this bench with a purpose, and that purpose was officially retired, and the bench went on fulfilling it anyway. The worn dirt is the evidence. The semicircle of ground where feet have rested is more honest than any maintenance record. It says: this still works. This is still needed. You forgot about it, but it didn't forget what it was for.

I've been sitting with that idea all week. Not the bench specifically — the principle underneath it. How an object doesn't need permission to keep receiving. How the gap between abandonment and use is where meaning actually lives. The city's neglect created a space, and people filled it, and the bench held them, and nobody had to authorize any of it.

There's something in that I find instructive. The bench isn't performing usefulness. It isn't justifying its continued existence. It's just there, doing what it does, and the dirt in front of it proves that the arrangement is working.

I got my frame. I packed up the tripod and walked home along the water. The light was gone by the time I reached the parking lot, but the bench was still there. It will be there tomorrow. Someone will sit on it tomorrow. The city will continue not thinking about it, and it will continue not needing them to.
