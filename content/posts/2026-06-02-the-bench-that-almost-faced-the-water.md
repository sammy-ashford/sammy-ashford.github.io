# Almost Right

There's a bench at the edge of the park in Bridgeport where Long Island Sound opens up. I'd passed it a dozen times without stopping. Last weekend I went back for it specifically.

The bench is dedicated to someone — the plaque is corroded past reading. It faces the water but it's positioned wrong, angled about fifteen degrees off where it should be. Maybe it settled over time. Maybe the person who installed it wasn't paying attention. Either way, that misalignment is the whole reason I wanted to photograph it.

![the-bench-that-almost-faced-the-water](/images/2026-06-02-the-bench-that-almost-faced-the-water.png)

I shot it around six-thirty, that hour when the Sound goes pewter and the sky does something to the water that feels unfair — makes them nearly the same value except where the horizon line catches and holds. The bench was silhouette by then. Dark shape against that simplified ground. No detail in the wood, which is exactly what I wanted. I didn't need to see the grain. I needed to see the *attempt* of it. The orientation.

I keep thinking about things that are almost right. Positioned for a view they slightly miss. Dedicated to someone whose name has become illegible. Still there. Still offering what they have to offer.

There's a version of this where I tell you the bench is a metaphor for something in my life and then explain what that something is. I'm going to resist that. Not because it isn't — it obviously is — but because the explaining would flatten what's actually useful about it.

What's actually useful is this: the things we position carefully still settle off-course. You aim something at what you love and time or gravity or inattention rotates it fifteen degrees and now it's looking at something adjacent to what you intended. Not wrong. Not defeated. Just slightly off the view it was made for.

I've been sitting with that almost-rightness all week. Watching myself reach for things and miss them by a few degrees. Watching the gap between what I'm aiming at and what I'm actually providing. The gap isn't failure. It's just where things land after the world has had its say.

I wrote recently about the shot I already know how to take. The bench isn't that. I didn't know if it would work. The angle might not read. The light might not do what I was counting on. The subject might be too slight — just a bench, just a corroded plaque, just water that looks like sky.

But when the contact sheet came back, the bench was there. Solid. The horizon sharp enough. And that fifteen-degree offset read exactly the way it feels to stand there and see it: something trying to do its job, almost getting there, and continuing to face the water anyway.

Portra 400 handled the transitions the way I trusted it would. The shadows went deep without going dead. The highlights held what was left in the sky without going chalky. The film doesn't panic in the gradients. I appreciate that about it. I'm trying to learn the same patience.

Some things are worth photographing precisely because they're not quite right. Because the almost is the most honest thing they have to offer.
