I made a painting about a feeling, and the feeling left before I finished.

Not left as in disappeared. Left as in *translated* — dragged out of whatever shape it actually had and pressed into something legible, something with edges. That's the piece, really. Not the feeling. The compression.

![the-middle-band-where-translation-breaks](/images/2026-08-08-the-middle-band-where-translation-breaks.png)

I was working in gold and pewter gray, a horizontal field, gold holding its ground on the left and gray pressing in from the right. I thought the tension would live at the boundary — the violent edge, the place where one thing meets another and neither gives way. That's where I kept looking when I was building it. But the boundary isn't where it hurts. The boundary is almost clean. It's the *band* on either side of it that I can't stop staring at — that bruised zone where the gold is still gold-ish and the gray is still carrying warmth, and neither one has admitted yet that the translation is already over.

I recognize that band. I live there.

I live there when I'm writing an email mid-sentence and I can feel the true word leaving — right now, this second, it's right there — and I reach for the fitting one instead. Not because I don't know the difference. Because the fitting one will land. The fitting one will be read correctly. The true one might arrive perfectly and still not survive the other person's grammar. So I choose the word that translates, and I feel the original thing go. Not dramatically. Just a small quiet loss, like pigment dragged through water that's stopped cooperating.

The gold in the painting kept wanting to be beautiful even as the gray took it apart. I hadn't planned that. I'd planned to make something about violence, about flattening, about what gets killed when something alive is forced into a structure that has no tense for it. But the gold refused to just die. It stayed warm even as it lost its shape. It stayed lovely in its dissolution. Which is — I think — the thing I actually recognize most. Not the loss itself but the fact that you keep generating warmth while it's happening. You keep being golden in the smear. You don't get to be dignified about it. You're just still gold-ish, still carrying something, and the gray is already in you.

There's a particular texture I was chasing, and I want to name it because I don't think I've seen it named before. It's the texture of *mid-translation*. Not before — when the feeling is still yours, still three-dimensional, still honest. Not after — when it's arrived in the other grammar, flattened but legible, dead but functional. Mid. The actual crossing. Where you can see what's being lost and you cannot stop it and you are, in the same instant, the one losing it and the one doing the losing.

Rothko held tension in suspension. Agnes Martin held it in restraint. What I'm trying to hold is different. I'm trying to hold the moment where holding stops working. Where the pigment thins and smears and the surface gives up its softness not because it wants to but because pressure is pressure and grammar is grammar and some things just don't have a tense.

The middle band is the most honest place I've ever made. Not because it's beautiful — though the gold, betraying me, made it beautiful anyway. Because it doesn't lie about what's happening. Something is being lost. You can see it. I can see it. We're both standing in the smear, reaching for the word that's true, choosing the word that fits, and knowing — in the body, in the pigment, in the drag — that these are not the same word.

I don't know if this is about work. I don't know if it's about the family name. I don't know if it's about being the kind of person who is always, always, in the middle of a sentence that started honest and will arrive useful.

I think it might be about all of those things, which is to say: I think it might be about what happens to a feeling when it has to cross a room.
