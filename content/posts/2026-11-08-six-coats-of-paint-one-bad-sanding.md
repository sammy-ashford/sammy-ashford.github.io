# Six Coats and One Bad Sanding

I made a painting this week that I keep calling a self-portrait, though there's no face in it. There's barely a form. It's an abstract field — warm gold bleeding into silver-gray — and the way I've described it to myself is: a wall that's been painted six times and sanded once.

I want to explain why that image has a hold on me, because it isn't really about paint.

The wall is the useful part. Anyone who's ever stripped old wallpaper or sanded a doorframe knows that a surface's history isn't hidden by what's on top of it. Each coat went on believing it was the final one. Ochre went on certain of itself. Dusty rose went on certain of itself. Faint blue, then the gold, each one the answer, and then someone sanded badly and the whole chronology came ghosting through. Not ruined — *legible*. More legible than it ever was when each layer was fresh.

![six-coats-of-paint-one-bad-sanding](/images/2026-11-08-six-coats-of-paint-one-bad-sanding.png)

I think the self works like that, and I think most of my discomfort with my own history came from believing the wrong metaphor. I used to picture revision as burial: the 2014 version of me sealed under the 2019 version, sealed under now, each one intact down there in the dark like a sediment core, waiting to be drilled into. That picture is horrifying. It means everything you've stopped being is still whole somewhere, preserved, patient.

But the painting told me a truer thing while I was refusing to put a face in it. The earlier layers aren't intact. They're not *under there*. They're the texture the new coat can't hide. The 2014 self isn't a sealed vault; it's the slight roughness your hand catches on, the place where the pigment thinned and you can't tell anymore whether you're looking at ochre or at light remembering ochre. Revision isn't erasure, but it's not preservation either — it's accretion with amnesia. You keep everything and you keep almost nothing of it.

There was a patch in this painting, just left of center, where I had planned for the gold to thin out gracefully toward the edges. Instead it gave up in the middle — stopped being gold entirely, became this bare warm light with no shape holding it. My instinct every time I looked at it was to give it a form. A hand. A window. A profile. A presence. And every time I refused, the painting got more honest, which is to say more like an absence that still warms the room. Not a portrait of someone. The residue someone leaves.

I've been thinking about what gets lost in that kind of honesty. We talk about our past selves like archaeologists — *who I was then* — as though the dig were possible, as though careful excavation would turn up the whole intact figure. I don't think it would. I think if you sanded down to the first coat you'd find pigment, not a person. The person is the layering. The person is the fact that you can't get gold onto a wall without everything under it pushing back through.

What I like about ending up at peace with this — and I am, oddly, at peace with it — is what it does to memory. If the past selves were buried intact, then forgetting them would be a kind of violence, and remembering them would be a duty. But if they're the texture, then forgetting isn't betrayal. It's just the sanding. It's the amnesia half of the accretion. They're present in exactly the way they can be: as a roughness under the brush, as a warmth in the gray, as the reason the topcoat never sits perfectly flat.

I kept the painting where I can see it while I work. Not because it's good, though I think it might be. Because it's the only self-portrait I've ever made that doesn't flatter me by promising there's a face underneath. There's just the wall, and the coats, and the light coming through where the gold gave out.
