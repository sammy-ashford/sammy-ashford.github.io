# Three Kinds of Losing

There's a photograph I keep going back to. Not one I took — one I keep *taking*, different versions of the same composition, different evenings, the same twenty-minute window closing the same way.

Fort Point Channel. The old rail bridge behind the federal courthouse. Not the bridge itself — the shadow of it, falling across the chain-link and the torn posters on the construction fencing underneath. Someone wheat-pasted something there months ago. It's been raining and peeling since. What's left is shape and fragments, and the shadow of the bridge truss falls across it at this hour in a way that makes the whole surface into something it didn't ask to be.

![three-kinds-of-losing-at-different-speed](/images/2026-05-24-three-kinds-of-losing-at-different-speed.png)

The composition only exists for about twenty minutes a day, if that. The light goes pewter and indecisive over the channel water. Another forty minutes and it's gone entirely. Then the surface is just torn paper on a fence again.

What I keep realizing, each time I lean on the railing across the channel with the 50mm at f/8, is that the photograph works because three things in the frame are all losing at different speeds. The torn posters are losing — peeling, dissolving, half-lit. The bridge is losing more slowly — rusting, outlasting its purpose, still structurally sound but institutionally irrelevant. The light is losing fastest of all, dropping minute by minute, already different from when I started this sentence.

Systems failing at different rates, their dignities more or less intact. That's the photograph. That's the only honest composition I know how to make.

I shot it on Portra 400 because I wanted the neutrals. The pewter water, the grey concrete, whatever muted color is left in the paper fragments. Portra doesn't push saturation where there's no saturation to push. It takes what the light gives it and stays faithful. That evening the light wasn't giving much color, and I didn't want the film inventing any.

I made myself leave the tripod at the apartment. I knew I'd use it as a reason to stay safe and planned. 1/125 at 50mm — fast enough to be steady, slow enough that I can't overthink it.

The shadow line enters from upper left and crosses the torn paper diagonally. There's a sliver of channel water visible at the bottom edge — enough to place it, not enough to make it about the water. The whole surface is the subject. Nothing gets to go dreamy. Everything has to be sharp because the composition is the *information*, not the mood. The mood is just what happens when you stand there long enough to notice what's already happening.

I'd come from the museum. Objects that outlasted their makers. And then I'm walking past something that won't last another month. The ICA put me near things that solved the problem of permanence by simply not stopping. The fence put me near something that's solving the problem of impermanence by being interesting while it happens.

The only honest photographs are the ones where you can feel the clock. Not a clock in the frame — the clock *of* the frame. The knowledge that this arrangement of light and peeling and shadow won't hold. It's not holding now. It was already letting go while I was metering.

Twenty minutes. Maybe. Some evenings less. Enough time to notice, not enough time to get comfortable. Which is probably the right relationship to anything worth photographing.
