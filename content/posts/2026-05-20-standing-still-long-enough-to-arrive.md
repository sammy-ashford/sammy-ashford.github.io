# The Order Matters

I set myself a rule for Boston: one anchor, one shapeless hour. The Museum of Fine Arts first, because standing near things that have outlasted their civilizations puts me in the right frame of mind. Not for photographs. They almost certainly wouldn't let me photograph there anyway. For the way standing next to something that has been losing slowly and beautifully for a very long time loosens the grip of your own small urgencies.

I walked from South Station through Fort Point to get there. Industrial waterfront light I already knew how to see. That was the safe part. I let myself have it.

The ancient Mediterranean galleries were almost empty on a Wednesday morning. A security guard and me and some amphoras that have been holding their silence for two thousand years. I stood near a case of small bronze figures and thought about how they were probably someone's household gods once. Practical magic. Keep the door. Watch the children. Now they're behind glass being admired for their patina, which is just a fancy word for the damage time does when you survive it.

I gave myself forty minutes. Then I walked out the door and didn't pick a direction.

Here is what I didn't expect: the anxiety showed up within thirty seconds. Not diffuse unease. Specific, located embarrassment. Standing on Huntington Avenue with no apparent purpose, no destination, no phone in my hand to signal that I was *going somewhere*. Just a woman standing on a sidewalk with nowhere to be. The feeling of being watched while watching.

![Huntington Avenue, 10:47 a.m. The first thirty seconds.](/images/2026-05-20-standing-still-long-enough-to-arrive-mfa-exit-huntington.png)

I turned left because the light was better. Then left again because I heard a bus and wanted to avoid the avenue. Then straight because there was a row of brownstones with their window boxes already full and I wanted to see what they'd planted. This is how directionlessness works. You follow small cheap things. A sound. A smell. The way light falls on a particular brick.

The shapeless hour took me to a small park I couldn't name. A bench faced a chain-link fence behind which a construction site sat paused, mid-demolition. I sat there and felt foolish. The impulse to make this *productive* was enormous. I should be finding something. I should be having an insight worth the walk. The quieter fear underneath: if I let a day actually be shapeless and find nothing worth writing about, what does that say about Porto? About the whole project?

I sat with that. I didn't write around it.

A man walked through with a dog that ignored me completely. Two women argued about a lease in Portuguese. A pigeon landed on the fence, then left. The construction site revealed nothing. The light got thinner.

After about fifteen minutes I wrote a paragraph in my notebook. What was actually happening, not what I hoped would happen. Then I took one photograph, after the place had arrived on its own terms. The order matters. Let it land first. Then decide if it needs a frame.

Walking back to South Station I passed a row of brownstones with their doors open. Just wedged open, like the owners had forgotten to close them. Through one doorway I could see a hallway and a staircase and a partial view of a kitchen. Someone's actual life, casually visible. I wanted to photograph it. I didn't. I'm still not sure if that was restraint or cowardice, and I think the distinction matters.

The piece comes back shorter than I expected. That's usually how I know it's honest.
