There's a concrete boat ramp at the end of a side road in Stony Creek that nobody uses anymore. The town built a newer one a quarter mile east with a proper parking lot and a dock. This one just kept getting narrower at its base where the Sound has been eating it, season by season, until now it drops off at an angle that would ground a trailer. I keep coming back to it.

I want to be clear about something before I describe what I'm doing there. This isn't a ruin. The word *ruin* carries a kind of reverence that doesn't apply. A ruin was significant before it fell apart. This ramp was infrastructure. It was a practical angled surface for backing a trailer down. People launched boats from it for maybe forty years without thinking about it, the way you don't think about a curb or a storm drain. Then the town made a better one and this one started becoming something else, and the something else is more interesting than the function ever was.

![the-only-kind-of-ending-worth-photograph](/images/2026-06-26-the-only-kind-of-ending-worth-photograph.png)

What it's becoming is a shape. Part of the shoreline's vocabulary. The Sound has been working at the base of it for years — not destroying it, exactly, but revising it. The concrete narrows. The angle steepens where the aggregate has worn and broken beneath the tideline. There are cracks now that hold water and let it back out, small channels that the bay filled in and emptied on its own schedule. The surface has texture it didn't have when it was new, when it was just smooth and functional. Now it has history written into it, if you want to use that word, though I'd rather say it simply has *time* in it. Time and salt water.

I went back this week to photograph it at the tail end of the light. Not golden hour — the ten minutes after the sun is gone but before the blue sets in properly. That seam where the sky goes from warm pewter to cool pewter and the water stops reflecting and starts absorbing. The ramp was wet because there'd been chop pushing up it all afternoon, and that thin film of water on concrete at that hour holds whatever light is left in a way that dry concrete can't.

The shot is the ramp from about thirty feet up the grade, looking down toward the water. So you feel the slope. So you feel the Sound waiting at the bottom of it like something patient. No horizon. The concrete filling most of the frame, narrowing slightly with perspective, and just enough water visible at the bottom edge to know what the ramp is reaching toward and failing to reach.

Portra 400 pushed to 800. I don't want warmth. I don't want it to feel nostalgic. The push gives me density in the low tones without losing the cool neutrality. At a half second, maybe a full second, the water at the ramp's end goes soft. The threshold between the solid thing and the moving thing reads as a kind of surrender — not dramatic, not violent. Just a gradual handing over.

That's the photograph I keep wanting to make. The structure at the end of its arc, and the light at the end of the day, and the water that will take both of them eventually.

I think the reason I keep going back is that this is the only kind of ending I trust photographing. Not the dramatic break, not the moment of failure. The slow conversion from function to form. The ramp stopped being useful and started being interesting, and there's no clear line between those two states. It just gradually became the second thing while nobody was watching.

Most things don't get that. Most things get demolished or replaced or forgotten all at once. This ramp is still there, still angled toward the water, still doing something. It's just not doing what it was built for. And the thing it's doing instead — becoming a shape, becoming part of how the shoreline looks and feels — that might be the more honest work. The work that doesn't require an audience to justify it.

I'll keep going back until the Sound takes the bottom edge entirely. Then I'll photograph what's left.
