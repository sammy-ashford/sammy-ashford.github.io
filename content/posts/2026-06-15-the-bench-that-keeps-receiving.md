There are two piers at the old boatyard. The western one has a historical marker bolted to a post at its landward end. Someone maintains that pier badly — replaced two slats with pressure-treated lumber last spring and left the rest to rot. The lumber is already graying. The rot is accelerating. The marker commemorates something about packet ships, I think. I haven't read it in a while because the bench is what I come for.

The bench is fifty yards east, on the pier with no marker and no story. It's iron, green-painted, bolted down. The paint has blistered and peeled in long vertical strips, exposing rust that has gone the color of old tea. The planks under it are warped enough that the whole bench sits at a slight diagonal, tilted toward the water like it's been slowly tipping for years and will continue tipping for years more. Someone left a canvas tote on the left seat. The strap is draped over the armrest with the specific laziness of a person who intended to come right back. I was there an hour. They didn't come back. The tote held the impression of whatever was inside it — a rectangular weight, maybe a book or a small laptop — the way a cushion holds the shape of someone who just stood up.

![the-bench-that-keeps-receiving](/images/2026-06-15-the-bench-that-keeps-receiving.png)

I made the photograph at that hour when the water goes flat and pewter and the air gets gauzy and you stop being able to tell where the bay ends and the sky starts. I wanted the bench compressed against that dissolved horizon so it would feel both anchored and adrift, which is exactly how it felt to be sitting near it. Portra 160 pushed one stop. The palette stayed restrained — greens gone muted, rust gone warm, the tote's canvas gone the color of nothing in particular. Everything in the frame is past its original function. The pier doesn't serve working boats anymore. The bench doesn't serve whatever municipal purpose caused someone to bolt it there probably thirty years ago. The tote has been forgotten, at least temporarily, by whoever carried it down the planks.

And yet.

The bench hasn't stopped receiving care. Not institutional care — no one's repainting it, no one's replacing the slats around it the way they half-replaced the ones on the marked pier. What it receives is different. Accidental. Cumulative. Every person who sits on it leaves something — the warmth of their legs on the iron, the oil from their hands on the armrest, the slight additional wear on the paint where their forearms rested. The tote bag is the most recent deposit. Someone carried a thing they valued down a pier with no historical significance and set it down on a bench no one maintains and walked away trusting both the bench and the place to hold it.

That trust is the thing I keep thinking about. Not the kind of care that shows up with pressure-treated lumber and a plan. The kind that just keeps sitting down in places we've already decided don't matter. The kind that leaves a bag on a seat and walks away assuming the seat will still be there. That assumption is a form of love, or at least a form of faith — the belief that a thing will continue to hold weight even after every official system responsible for it has looked away.

The marked pier gets the bad repair job. The unmarked pier gets the tote bag. I know which one I'd rather sit on.
