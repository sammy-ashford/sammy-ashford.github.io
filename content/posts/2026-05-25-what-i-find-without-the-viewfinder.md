# The Wound That Doesn't Want to Heal

I spent an hour yesterday morning at the Central Artery excavation near Haymarket, and I need to tell you about the chainlink first because that's what I would do if I were being careful about this.

The chainlink is galvanized, weathered to the color of old nickel, and it separates you from a pit that used to be an elevated highway. The Big Dig took the highway down and put it underground and what's left above ground is this — a gash that Boston has been trying to dress for twenty years. Steel beams mid-demolition. Concrete rubble sorted into piles that suggest someone has a plan for them. The geometry is irresistible if you shoot structural decay the way I do, which is to say reverently. I loaded Portra into the M6 and shot it wide enough to lift the iron out of the gray and I don't regret a single frame.

That was the planned anchor. One hour. Permission to do what I'm already good at.

Then I put the camera in my bag and walked for an hour with no destination and no frame.

![Central Artery excavation, Haymarket. Portra on the M6.](/images/2026-05-25-what-i-find-without-the-viewfinder-chainlink-excavation-haymarket.png)

Here is what I want to tell you honestly. The second hour was worse. Not because nothing happened — a man asked me for directions to the North End, a woman was walking a three-legged dog very slowly along the Greenway, someone had left a single lawn chair in the middle of the plaza outside the haymarket building as if they'd meant to return. These are not nothing. These are exactly the kind of details I would have organized into a composition if I'd let myself reach for the viewfinder.

But I didn't reach for it. And what I learned in that hour is that my eye works about three seconds faster than my nerve. I see the frame. I feel the pull. And then I feel something else — a tightening in my throat, a half-step backward, a voice that says *you're not invisible, they can see you seeing them.* Usually I solve that by backing up to the 90mm and calling it restraint. Without the camera I couldn't solve it at all. I just had to stand there being looked at.

A three-legged dog does not care if you have a camera. That helped.

The point of this rehearsal wasn't to abandon what I'm good at. The point was to make sure what I'm good at isn't the only thing that happens in Porto. And what I'm bringing back from this morning is a working document that contains, among other things, proof that I can stay in a room without the FM2 mediating. It was uncomfortable. I wrote *I don't know how to be somewhere without the camera organizing the world for me* in my notes and then I sat on a bench and watched the excavation site just exist without me framing it and it was fine. The wound kept being a wound. The chainlink kept catching light. None of it needed me to make it legible.

One moment where the camera stayed in the bag and I stayed in the room: the lawn chair. I stood near it for maybe four minutes. No one came back for it. The sun moved across the plastic in a way that would have been beautiful on Portra. I didn't shoot it. I just watched the light change and let that be enough.

This document goes into the Porto packet as a diagnostic. If it tells me I need a different structure for the unscripted blocks, I'll rewrite those blocks. If it tells me the planned anchor was the easy part — and it was — then at least I know that now.
