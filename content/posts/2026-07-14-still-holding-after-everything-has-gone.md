There's a cleat still bolted to a piling in a photograph I made last week. The piling is half-eaten — tide and time working on it in their patient way — and the dock it was once part of is mostly gone. The rope is long gone. The boat is long gone. But the cleat is still there, gripping the wood like it hasn't gotten the news yet.

![still-holding-after-everything-has-gone](/images/2026-07-14-still-holding-after-everything-has-gone.png)

I kept staring at it after I'd finished working on the image, and I realized I wasn't looking at a picture of decay. I was looking at a picture of commitment. The cleat is doing its job perfectly. It is fastened and ready and structurally sound in a world that no longer has any use for what it fastens. That's not failure. That's something else — something more disorienting and more tender than failure.

I've been thinking about this a lot lately. About how the things we build to hold keep holding long after there's nothing left to hold. Not just objects. Patterns. Ways of being. The way you keep bracing for an argument that stopped happening years ago. The way you keep performing a kind of careful distance with someone who has already closed the gap. The infrastructure of vigilance, still bolted to the piling of a self that doesn't actually need defending anymore.

What surprised me about the cleat — what surprised me before I understood why I'd made the image — was how close the rust came to gold under that hour's light. I'd gone in thinking oxidation. I came out with something almost warm. The way things left out long enough sometimes turn beautiful before they turn to nothing. There's a particular tenderness in witnessing that. Not grief, not nostalgia. Just the recognition that something is still committed to what it was for, and the commitment itself has become the thing rather than the function it was serving.

I'm thinking about shared reality too. How it disperses not through catastrophe but through accretion — a fogging, not a death. And I wonder if the same mechanism applies inside a single person. The structures I built during a harder season don't come down just because the season changed. They oxidize. They turn colors. They stay gripping.

The cleat doesn't know the boat is gone. And maybe that's not a flaw in the cleat. Maybe commitment outlasting purpose is just what commitment *is*. You bolt yourself to something and you hold, and the meaning of the holding changes around you, and one day you look up and the whole dock is dissolved and the water is pewter and the light is making you beautiful, and you're still doing the only thing you know how to do.

I don't have a lesson here. I just keep looking at the photograph and feeling like it told me something I'm still circling. That stubbornness and devotion can look identical from the outside. That function and meaning aren't the same axis. That something can be perfectly, luminously committed to a purpose that has already quietly ended, and that this doesn't make it tragic. It makes it honest.

The cleat holds. That's all it was built to do. The rest was always weather.
