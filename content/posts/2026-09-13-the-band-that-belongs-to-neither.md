# The Tideline Band

I went back to the pier off Old Quarry Road last night, in the last hour before dusk, because I'd written myself into a corner. The scumbled margin — the place where one thing bleeds into another and neither wins — had become, over a few days of writing, a metaphor again. And I've started to distrust that move. Pigments contaminating each other, translation breaking down at the middle band: all true, all felt, but metaphors have a way of letting you off the hook. You describe the thing in terms of another thing and both stay abstract.

So I wanted the physical fact. It's on the pilings. Each one carries a band about eight inches wide where the tide has spent its whole working life: dark above where the wood stays wet, dark below where it stays submerged longer still, and in between that strip that belongs to neither condition. Not wet. Not dry. Stained by both. Twice a day the water passes through it in each direction and never claims it.

![the-band-that-belongs-to-neither](/images/2026-09-13-the-band-that-belongs-to-neither.png)

Standing in front of it, I understood something I'd been circling without landing. Contamination — the state of being worked on by two things at once, marked by both, owned by neither — isn't a way of describing in-between-ness. It's a way of life. The band isn't transitioning from wet to dry. It isn't going anywhere. It persists precisely as ambiguous, and the persistence is the point. The tide will keep passing through it long after the pier has forgotten what it was for.

Because that's the other thing about this pier. It stopped short of any far shore years ago. Nobody lands anything off the end anymore except the guy who leaves a chair tipped against the bait shack door every morning — evidence of someone recently present, which I kept in frame at the far edge, because a portrait of ambiguity shouldn't pretend to be unpeopled. The structure outlasted its purpose and the water keeps making it into something else, slowly, without malice. There's no drama in it. The pilings are just going soft, and the tideline band is the record of that ongoing revision.

I shot it the way you'd talk to someone you respect: 50mm, no wider, because a wide angle would dramatize the decay and this isn't a ruin shot. f/8, so the foreground band holds sharp while the rest of the arc drifts. 1/60th, slow enough that the water smears against the wood — the boundary scumbling in the frame the way it does in everything else. Portra pushed a stop, because the Sound goes pewter around 7:15 this time of year and I wanted film that would hold that strange color instead of correcting it. Hard light would have given the pilings an edge they haven't earned.

I allowed myself a second frame at 1/15th, where the waterline dissolves further, almost entirely. I haven't decided which one I mean yet. If the dissolved one is the truer picture, that tells me something uncomfortable about what I actually believe — that the margin doesn't hold, that everything eventually smears. But I don't think that's it. The band on the piling has been dissolving and re-forming for decades. It's still there. Ambiguity that persists isn't the same as ambiguity that fails to resolve.

I've been thinking about this in the context of the week, too — the parts of my life where I'm stained by conditions that don't sum to a single state. Competence and its performance. Attention that is both given and received. The channels I thought were separate, running into each other twice daily without malice, leaving a mark that belongs to neither and outlasts whatever I thought I was there to do.

That's what the pier taught me, standing there while the light lost its conviction: you don't have to resolve the band. You just have to let the tide keep its appointment.
