# The Sill Makes the Case

The lock says nobody gets in. The brushes say somebody still comes back. I've been thinking all week about that argument, and how the whole of it fits on a window sill the width of a forearm.

Old Saybrook Point, 6:40 in the evening. There's a bait shack at the end of a gravel lane that's been closed since the state re-did the launch ramp — padlock rusted into the hasp, but someone still keeps a coffee can of brushes on the sill. That's the photograph. Not the shack whole, not the lane, not the pier. The sill: the can, the padlock, the paint peeling off the door frame in long ribbons that have curled back and stuck to themselves. Human absent, recently present. You could stand there for an hour and never resolve which of the two objects is telling the truth, because both of them are.

![brushes-on-the-sill-padlock-gone-to-rust](/images/2026-09-21-brushes-on-the-sill-padlock-gone-to-rust.png)

What keeps pulling me back is the paint. Layers of green over gray over primer-red, each coat failing to fully cover the last. The standard reading is decay — neglect, salt air, time winning. But standing where the brush-person would stand, forty minutes before sunset, I couldn't see it that way. The light at that hour does something specific: the Sound goes pewter and the shadows fill in rather than sharpen, and peeling paint loses its document quality and becomes surface. Almost pigment. The green doesn't read as a failed covering of the gray; it reads as a layer in conversation with it. The primer-red showing through at the curls isn't damage to the green. It's the green's honesty.

This is the same argument I keep having with myself about scumbling — one layer intruding on another, the underpaint refusing to stay buried. I wrote about contamination in pigments a while back, the way a stubborn undertone shifts everything laid over it. The door frame is that, at architectural scale. Each generation of paint tried to say *this is the color now*, and each one failed incompletely, and the failures stacked into strata. The frame isn't ruined. It's a record of its own attempts.

I want to insist on the distinction, because it matters to me: accretion is not decay wearing a nicer shirt. Decay is subtraction — material leaving. Accretion is addition that never quite cancels. The padlock is decay; the rust has eaten the mechanism and the mechanism is gone. But the paint is accretion; nothing has left the door frame, it's all still there, red under gray under green, each layer legible in exactly the proportion that it failed to be erased. The sill holds both arguments at once, side by side, and refuses to adjudicate.

Technically it was a quiet setup. Hasselblad, 80mm — the normal view, no drama, standing where you'd stand if you were the person who left the brushes. Tripod, f/16 for front-to-back depth on the sill, which put me down around a fifteenth of a second at ISO 100. That shutter speed let the water behind the shack go slightly liquid where it enters the frame edge, and I decided that was fine, even good — the gauze belongs there, the way fog belongs at the edge of a remembered afternoon. Portra 160, because its palette in pewter light leans warm-neutral and holds separation in the shadowed peel where Ektar would go muddy or too red. One frame of the sill. One pull-back with the pilings entering frame left. Then the camera went away, before the light turned sentimental.

I've been noticing how much of what I love photographing lately is exactly this: evidence of repeated, incomplete covering. Paint over paint. A habit over an older habit. A way of talking to someone layered over an earlier way of talking to them, none of it fully opaque. The strata are the subject. The person who leaves the brushes knows the shack is locked and comes back anyway, and that returning is itself another coat — one that doesn't cover the abandonment, just sits on top of it, translucent, letting everything underneath show through a little.

The lock and the brushes will keep arguing as long as the sill holds them both. I don't want to pick a winner. I just wanted to stand where the argument is loudest and let it run.
